From Checkbox to Camera: How Cleveland's Lead Risk Assessment Standards Got Serious

July 21, 2026 · William M. Barker, LA 10055

Cleveland's lead-safe program is seven years old this year, and it has changed more in the last twenty months than in the five years before that. If you own rental property, manage a portfolio, or work as a local agent in charge (LAIC) for out-of-state owners, you've probably felt the requirements shift under your feet: new memos, new forms, new photo requirements, new pushback from the city on reports that used to sail through. This isn't the city being arbitrary. It's the closing of a loophole that has quietly shaped the market for years, and understanding how we got here tells you a lot about where Cleveland lead compliance is headed next.

Where It Started: A Law With No Teeth for Enforcement

Cleveland's lead safe law, Chapter 365 of the Codified Ordinances, enacted by Ordinance No. 747-2019, required every pre-1978 rental unit in the city to hold a Lead Safe Certificate, with the certification window opening March 1, 2021, and full compliance due by March 1, 2023. It was built on 33 policy recommendations from the Lead Safe Cleveland Coalition. On paper, it was a serious law. In practice, adoption crawled.

By early 2023, News 5 Cleveland reported that only about 20,000 of an estimated 103,000 rental units had certified, roughly one in five. By the end of that year, The Land and Signal Cleveland were tracking applications declining for five straight quarters. A March 2024 city presentation put single-family compliance at just 12 percent. The law existed. The market largely ignored it.

October 2024: The Clearance-Exam Loophole Closes

The turning point was Mayor Bibb's Executive Order 2024-03, signed October 14, 2024. The city's own FAQ on the change is blunt about why: 11 children had been lead-poisoned by peeling paint inside homes that already held a current Lead Safe Certificate. The certificates were real. The paint problems weren't being caught.

The reason was structural. Before October 18, 2024, a landlord could obtain a certificate with a clearance exam, a pass/fail dust-wipe test that only confirms a unit is clean at that moment. It doesn't determine whether lead hazards exist, only whether they've been controlled well enough to pass a swab. After the order, the city stopped accepting clearance exams to initiate a certificate and required a full lead risk assessment instead: a room-by-room visual evaluation of every painted surface, performed only by a licensed risk assessor, whose job is specifically to determine the existence, severity, and location of hazards, not just whether a floor happened to be clean the day someone tested it.

That single change moved the entire program from “did it pass a snapshot test” to “did a licensed professional actually look.”

2025: Closing the Documentation Gap

Requiring a risk assessment only matters if the resulting paperwork actually reflects what the assessor saw. The city's next move addressed exactly that. A November 5, 2025 memo from the Department of Public Health, Clarification on Proper Documentation for Visual Assessments, called out a pattern of reports listing broad, generalized entries (“all windows,” “all doors,” “all chewable surfaces”) instead of documenting each component individually by room, wall side, and condition, as required under OAC 3701-32-07(G)(3). Reports that didn't break findings down to the individual window, door, or trim piece started getting kicked back with “additional information required” notices rather than approved.

It's a small-sounding rule change with a large practical effect. A report that says “all windows: intact” and a report that documents each of the fourteen windows in a duplex individually can describe the same property and produce very different pictures of where the actual hazards are, and very different levels of confidence that nothing was missed. Anyone who has done this work for more than a few inspections knows there's real variation across the market in how far assessors go to document what they find. The city clearly noticed the same thing, because nearly every guidance update since has been aimed at closing that gap rather than opening new ones.

June 2026: The Photo-Documentation Era

That brings us to the most consequential update yet. On June 25, 2026, the city issued its Updates for Risk Assessment Report Requirements memo, effective July 10, 2026, and walked through it in detail at the June 18 RA Monthly Meeting. The headline change is photographic evidence, and it is comprehensive:

  • Exterior: clear, color, uncropped photos of all four building elevations, plus any garages or sheds, plus yard areas with exposed bare soil, and a photo taken while standing on the porch, showing the porch floor itself.
  • Interior: either a labeled photo of every room, or enough labeled photos to fully document every exclusion, hazard, and intact friction/impact/chewable surface claimed in the report. In a pre-1978 house, that second option effectively means every room anyway (those surfaces are everywhere), which is why we photograph every room as standard practice. Every deteriorated lead-based paint hazard identified must be photographed—no exceptions.
  • Floor plans: must match the visual assessment exactly, label every room including accessible basements and attics, and show where every dust sample was collected. As the city put it plainly at the June meeting: “If it's not on the floor plan, it does not exist for review purposes.”
  • Exclusions: limited to four defensible categories: uncoated, properly enclosed, new (post-1978) construction, or truly inaccessible. Each has its own documentation standard. The city even issued a companion guide on visually identifying post-1978 components, since builder's receipts and permits often don't exist decades later.
  • Lab data: the original, unmodified XRF export must now be included as an appendix, not just a summary table.

Put together, this closes the loop that clearance exams and vague narratives left open for years. A report can no longer describe a property in general terms and hope the description holds up. Every claim (every exclusion, every “intact” surface, every hazard finding) now has to be backed by a photo that lines up with a labeled floor plan and raw lab output the city can independently check. If an assessor calls a component “enclosed” or “new construction” without matching visual evidence, that gap is now visible to anyone reviewing the file, not just the assessor who wrote it.

The Cost Squeeze, and Why There's No Way Around It Anymore

None of this happens in a vacuum. A standard Ohio lead risk assessment typically runs anywhere from just under $500 to around $700 per unit. That's the easy part. If the assessment turns up hazards, the renovation, repair, and painting work needed to actually fix them can run from a few hundred dollars for a single deteriorated windowsill to tens of thousands of dollars for a property with widespread failing paint. That's a real range against a unit that might rent for around $1,000 a month. That math is exactly why compliance in Cleveland has been so uneven: Signal Cleveland reported that certified units actually dropped from roughly 31,000 in late 2023 to about 24,700 by April 2025, as thousands of existing certificates lapsed rather than renewed. By late 2025, roughly 28,000 units held active certification, still less than a third of the city's estimated rental stock, and the city has since floated loosening parts of the law, including longer certification intervals for lower-risk, consistently compliant properties, to keep more owners in the system rather than push them out of it. The coalition even wound down a program that had paid landlords $750 to $1,000 per unit to certify, redirecting that money toward actual remediation instead.

That's the real backdrop here: a market where the economics strain small landlords, and where some investors and LAICs I talk with have quietly stepped back from the Cleveland rental market altogether. It has also meant something nobody in this industry likes to say out loud:

The path of least resistance has sometimes looked less like fixing the property and more like finding an assessor willing to look past what's actually there. That option is closing fast.

Every guidance update since 2024 (component-level documentation, standardized exclusions, mandatory photos tied to a matching floor plan, raw lab data attached to every report) has made it progressively harder for a hazard to go undocumented, and progressively easier for the city to spot a report that doesn't hold up under review. The cost pressure on owners hasn't gone away. What's disappearing is the shortcut around it.

What This Means Going Forward

For risk assessors, the June 2026 requirements aren't really a new burden so much as a new floor. Anyone documenting properties this way already (full photo sets, component-by-component findings, floor plans that actually match what's in the report) won't notice much of a change in their workflow. Anyone who wasn't will find that additional-documentation requests, callbacks, and re-inspections are no longer occasional friction; they're the new baseline for anything that doesn't hold up to a photo-by-photo review. And under OAC 3701-32-04, misrepresentation and violations of Ohio's lead rules aren't just a paperwork problem; they're grounds for license suspension or revocation.

I've walked away from work rather than sign a report I couldn't defend down to the photo, and it has cost me relationships with owners and property managers who wanted a faster answer than the property actually supported. That's a hard trade in the short run. But the market the city is building toward, where every finding has to be photographed, mapped, and backed by raw data, is a market that rewards exactly the kind of documentation discipline we've built our process around from day one. Owners, property managers, and LAICs managing risk across a portfolio are going to need assessors who were already operating at this standard before it became mandatory, not ones scrambling to retrofit their process under a July 10 deadline.
