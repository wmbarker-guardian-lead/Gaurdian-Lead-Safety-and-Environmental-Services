What Is a Lead Clearance Exam and When Do You Need One?

June 8, 2026 · William M. Barker, LA 10055

A lead clearance exam answers one specific question: has the work that was done actually left the property safe to re-occupy? It is not an initial assessment of whether lead hazards exist. It is the confirmation step that comes after remediation, abatement, or renovation work—the test that tells you the job was done correctly and the dust has been addressed.

Cleveland landlords sometimes confuse a clearance exam with a lead risk assessment. They serve different purposes and are ordered at different points in the compliance process. Understanding the difference saves time, avoids redundant testing, and ensures you have the right documentation at the right moment.

What a Lead Clearance Exam Actually Tests

A clearance exam is a combined visual and quantitative procedure. It is not limited to checking for lead dust—it also confirms that remediation work was actually completed as specified, documents the condition of the property at the point of clearance, and establishes a written record that the unit is ready for re-occupancy. The scope of the exam depends on what work was done, how it was contained, and whether a lead hazard control order is involved.

Visual inspection. The examiner begins with a walkthrough of the clearance area, checking for deteriorated paint (peeling, chipping, chalking, or damaged surfaces), visible settled dust, paint chips, and debris. Interior floors, window sills, window troughs, and horizontal surfaces are all evaluated. Exterior surfaces are checked if exterior work was performed. If deteriorated paint or debris is present, the contractor must address it before dust sampling can proceed. This phase also serves as documentation that the work scope was completed—the examiner records the condition of the treated surfaces as part of the clearance report.

Dust wipe sampling. After the visual inspection, the examiner collects dust wipe samples from the required surfaces in the clearance area. The three required interior surfaces are the floor, the interior window sill (the horizontal ledge adjacent to the closed sash, sometimes called the window stool), and the window trough (the recessed channel where the sash slides, where lead dust and paint particles accumulate from years of friction). Samples are collected using standardized wipe methods and sent to an ODH-approved accredited laboratory for analysis.

Clearance area scope. The clearance area is not always limited to the rooms where work was performed. When interior work was done without dust containment, the clearance area expands to the entire residential unit—a minimum of four room equivalents must be sampled. When work was performed with proper dust containment, the clearance area is the containment zone plus a ten-foot buffer. The decision about containment during the renovation directly shapes how extensive the clearance exam needs to be. When a lead hazard control order has been issued by ODH or a local board of health, the order itself defines the required clearance scope, and the examiner must document that every hazard identified in the order has been addressed.

Additional sampling in specific cases. Standard clearance for indoor residential work focuses on the three dust wipe surfaces described above. Soil sampling may be required if exterior paint was disturbed using methods that could have spread contamination beyond the work area, or if bare soil remains after exterior abatement. When a full lead-based paint inspection by XRF identified specific surfaces, post-abatement clearance is coordinated against those findings. Clearance thresholds—the laboratory result levels that determine whether a unit passes or fails—are set by state and federal regulation and are subject to change; the applicable standard depends on the jurisdiction and the type of work performed.

Once all samples return from the laboratory within standards, the examiner issues a written clearance report documenting the visual findings, sample locations, laboratory results, and determination. This report is the formal record that the unit is cleared for occupancy and is an important document to retain in your property file.

When You Need a Lead Clearance Exam

There are three main situations where a clearance exam is required or strongly warranted for Cleveland rental properties.

After lead abatement. When a risk assessor identifies lead hazards and a contractor performs abatement—physically removing or encapsulating lead-based paint—a clearance exam is required before the unit can be re-occupied. This is not optional. Federal regulations under HUD's Lead Safe Housing Rule (24 CFR Part 35) require a clearance examination performed by an independent, licensed professional after all abatement activities. The assessor performing clearance must be different from the contractor who performed the work.

After interim controls. Interim controls are measures that manage lead hazards without full abatement—things like encapsulation, friction surface treatments, or specialized cleaning. Depending on the scope of work and the regulatory context, a clearance exam may be required after interim controls as well. When in doubt, document with a clearance exam. The cost of the exam is modest relative to the liability of re-occupying a unit with contaminated dust.

After renovation work under the RRP Rule. The EPA's Renovation, Repair, and Painting (RRP) Rule requires that renovation work in pre-1978 rental properties be performed by EPA-certified contractors using lead-safe work practices. After qualifying renovation work, a post-renovation cleaning verification is required. Depending on the scope, this may involve dust wipe testing. If the renovation disturbed more than six square feet of painted surface in any interior room, documentation of cleanup is required.

What a Clearance Exam Does Not Replace

A clearance exam is not a substitute for a lead risk assessment and does not satisfy Cleveland's requirement for a Lead Safe Certificate on its own.

Under Cleveland Ordinance 365, all pre-1978 rental properties must carry a valid Lead Safe Certificate, renewed every two years. That certificate comes from a full risk assessment—a process that includes visual inspection, paint chip sampling, and dust wipe testing throughout the unit, not just in areas where recent work was performed. If your property has never been assessed or if the certificate has expired, a clearance exam after renovation does not address that requirement.

The two exams complement each other. A risk assessment tells you the overall lead condition of the property. A clearance exam tells you whether a specific scope of work was completed effectively. If you are renovating a unit ahead of a lease renewal and your certificate is also expiring, it often makes sense to schedule both together: one site visit, coordinated timing, minimal disruption to your turnover schedule.

Who Can Perform a Lead Clearance Exam in Ohio

In Ohio, a clearance exam must be performed by a licensed risk assessor. The examiner must be independent from the contractor who performed the remediation or renovation—you cannot have the same firm do the work and sign off on the clearance. This separation exists to ensure the clearance result is an objective third-party verification, not a self-certification.

Licensing is administered through the Ohio Department of Health. When you schedule a clearance exam, ask for the assessor's ODH license number. Documentation of credentials should accompany the clearance report.

The Process and Timeline

A clearance exam typically takes one to two hours on-site for a standard rental unit. The assessor walks the work area, confirms that visible dust and debris have been removed, then collects dust wipe samples from the required surfaces. The unit should not be re-cleaned between the contractor finishing and the clearance visit—additional cleaning immediately before the exam can mask a problem that would otherwise show up in the samples.

Laboratory turnaround is typically two to three business days for standard processing. Rush processing is available when turnover timelines are tight. Once results come back clean, a written clearance report is issued documenting the sample locations, laboratory results, and the assessor's determination. Keep it with your property records—it may be requested during a city inspection or in the event of a tenant complaint.

If a sample fails, the contractor must re-clean the affected area and the clearance exam is repeated. The cost of a second visit is modest, but the delay reinforces why thorough initial cleanup by the renovation contractor matters. Before scheduling clearance, confirm that the contractor has performed a final HEPA vacuum and wet wipe of all surfaces in the work area.

Coordinating Clearance With Your Compliance Schedule

For landlords managing multiple units, a little coordination goes a long way. If you are planning summer renovation work on several properties, grouping clearance exams by neighborhood or building reduces scheduling overhead. We work with portfolio landlords regularly and can structure site visits to cover multiple clearance exams efficiently.

If you are also due for Lead Safe Certificate renewal on properties in the same area, a combined visit covering both risk assessment and clearance exam is often the most practical approach. We can discuss your specific situation, what work has been done, and what documentation you need before re-occupancy.

A clearance exam is the final quality check on renovation and remediation work. Done properly, it protects your tenants, documents your due diligence, and gives you a clean record for the property. If you have work wrapping up or scheduled for the coming weeks, build clearance into the project timeline from the start rather than treating it as an afterthought at move-in.
